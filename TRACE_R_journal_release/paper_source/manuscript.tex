\begin{abstract}
Road-inspection vehicles record camera settings, inertial motion, and vehicle state, yet restoration networks usually infer image corruption from pixels alone. This paper presents TRACE-R, a telemetry-conditioned restoration front end for road-defect detection. An exposure-centred capture record is converted into physically interpretable corruption evidence and fused with an image estimate according to sensor availability and reliability. The resulting state selects an internal restoration route and conditions lightweight spatial adapters throughout a shared backbone. The model returns one image and requires no inference-time degradation label. Under matched training and validation-only selection, TRACE-R is the strongest deployable method on two controlled road-damage benchmarks and achieves the highest primary AP50 on a native-resolution field sequence with synchronized camera and inertial records. Missing and deliberately inconsistent sensor-record interventions reduce accuracy, confirming that the gain depends on measured capture information rather than additional network capacity alone.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Image restoration, inertial sensing, multimodal perception, pavement inspection, road-defect detection.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{V}{ehicle-mounted} cameras can inspect long road corridors without placing personnel in traffic. Their usefulness, however, depends on the evidence delivered to the detector. Motion during exposure spreads narrow cracks over neighbouring pixels, defocus weakens pothole rims, and low illumination couples longer exposure with sensor noise. These effects can coexist, and an enhancement that looks pleasing can still remove the texture needed for recognition.

Road-damage research has improved detector scale, domain adaptation, and shape representation~\cite{arya2022rdd2022,lin2023dardd,li2024yoloxrdd,zhang2024shapeadaptation}. These advances generally assume that a usable image reaches the detector. Restoration can supply a front end, but blind restoration must infer both the corruption and the missing content from the same pixels. A soft pavement patch can be caused by camera rotation, focus error, or weak illumination; applying the wrong correction can amplify shadows or erase a crack.

Road-inspection vehicles already measure part of the missing cause. Camera exposure, gain, and focus state describe image formation; inertial samples describe motion during exposure; vehicle and timing records indicate whether those measurements are trustworthy. Such measurements are available before any defect label is produced. The opportunity is therefore not merely to append metadata to an image network, but to convert asynchronous and sometimes incomplete records into corruption evidence that can be checked against the image.

Restoration for inspection also has a different objective from photographic enhancement. A recovered image must remain faithful to the scene while preserving the features that support localization and classification. Pixel loss alone can smooth a narrow crack, whereas an unconstrained detector loss can reward visually unstable texture. The restoration and task signals must therefore be coordinated rather than treated as interchangeable quality measures.

An earlier study, RMR-P~\cite{ghorbani2026rmrp}, provided initial evidence that capture metadata can help pavement-image restoration. Its fixed summary code, however, could not represent motion during an exposure, distinguish a missing measurement from a genuine zero, or decide when visual evidence should override an unreliable record. \tracer advances that idea into a capture-aware and task-aware restoration framework. It aligns the measurements to each exposure, converts them into evidence for specific corruption causes, reconciles this evidence with an image estimate, and conditions a shared restorer at several spatial scales. During training, a frozen road-defect detector supplies feature and localization guidance while a dominant fidelity term constrains the returned image. The deployed system still produces one restored image and requires neither a degradation label nor a manual metadata mode.

The principal contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an exposure-centred capture-state formulation that turns camera, inertial, and vehicle records into cause-specific evidence while representing partial availability, uncertainty, and disagreement with the image;
  \item a backbone-independent conditioning mechanism that combines image and sensor evidence, preserves the image-only route when a measurement is unsupported, and adapts restoration throughout the feature hierarchy;
  \item a task-aware training objective and staged optimization schedule that preserve paired image structure while using frozen detector features and supervised detector evidence to protect defect cues; and
  \item matched controlled tests, direct sensor-record interventions, and a native-resolution field study based on the new Collingwood Road Inspection Dataset (CRID), which comprises synchronized high-resolution imagery and vehicle telemetry.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Automated road inspection has progressed from mobile-camera detection~\cite{maeda2018road} to multi-country benchmarks such as RDD2022~\cite{arya2022rdd2022}. Recent systems address cross-country domain shift~\cite{lin2023dardd}, rare defects~\cite{su2022fsrdd}, front-view acquisition~\cite{li2024yoloxrdd}, irregular damage geometry~\cite{zhang2024shapeadaptation}, shadows~\cite{kong2025shadowrobust}, and crack connectivity~\cite{jing2025topologycrack}. These studies improve recognition after an image has been captured. Low-light road-scene work confirms that acquisition quality can affect semantic perception~\cite{wang2022sfnetn}, but the camera image is still usually treated as the complete observation.

Image restoration provides a possible front end. Corruption-specific networks have been followed by models that share parameters across several degradations. Restormer uses efficient attention for high-resolution restoration~\cite{zamir2022restormer}, NAFNet establishes a strong activation-free baseline~\cite{chen2022nafnet}, and AirNet learns a degradation representation from the input image~\cite{li2022airnet}. PromptIR uses learned prompts~\cite{potlapalli2023promptir}; InstructIR follows a natural-language instruction~\cite{conde2024instructir}; DFPIR perturbs features according to a degradation prompt to reduce interference between restoration tasks~\cite{tian2025dfpir}; and DeMoE routes image features through degradation-specialized experts~\cite{feijoo2025demoe}. These methods offer increasingly capable shared restorers, but their corruption decision is obtained from pixels or an externally supplied condition rather than measured image-formation data.

A second line of work asks whether restoration helps recognition. TogetherNet couples enhancement and detection~\cite{wang2022togethernet}, visual-recognition-driven restoration recovers intrinsic semantic features~\cite{yang2023vrdir}, SR4IR introduces a task-driven perceptual loss and cross-quality training for recognition~\cite{kim2024tdp}, and UniRestore balances perceptual and task-oriented restoration with a diffusion prior~\cite{chen2025unirestore}. This literature establishes that PSNR and downstream accuracy can disagree. It also motivates a constraint that is central here: detector guidance should shape restoration without replacing a paired-image fidelity anchor.

External sensing addresses a different ambiguity by observing part of the capture process itself. Inertial-assisted deblurring constrains camera motion during exposure~\cite{joshi2010imu,hu2016smartphoneimu,yang2025gyro}, while event-guided methods use a separate high-temporal-resolution stream~\cite{kim2022eventexposure,kim2024cmta}. These methods mainly target motion blur and generally assume that the auxiliary stream is available and usable. Road-inspection records are less tidy: camera, inertial, and vehicle measurements arrive at different rates, may be partly absent, and provide evidence for different corruptions. No prior approach considered here jointly reconciles such records with visual corruption evidence, conditions a multi-degradation restorer, and optimizes the resulting image for pavement detection. That combined gap is the focus of \tracer.

\section{TRACE-R Method}
TRACE-R separates a practical question into four operations. It first aligns the measurements that belong to an exposure. It then forms one corruption estimate from the image and another from the supported measurements. A reliability-aware posterior reconciles the two estimates, and lightweight adapters use that posterior throughout a restoration backbone. Training finally constrains the returned image by both scene fidelity and road-defect evidence. This order is important: the network never receives a command such as ``apply motion deblurring.'' It receives an image and the records produced while that image was captured.

Let $I_d\in[0,1]^{3\times H\times W}$ be the observed road image and $I_c$ its clean target when paired training data are available. Measurements associated with the exposure are grouped as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}=\{m_C,M_I,m_V\},
\label{eq:packetgroups}
\end{equation}
where $m_C$ contains camera settings, $M_I$ is an inertial trajectory, and $m_V$ contains vehicle and synchronization context. The deployed mapping is
\begin{equation}
I_r=f_\theta(I_d,\mathcal{M}),
\label{eq:system}
\end{equation}
where $I_r$ is the only image supplied to the road-defect detector. The same mapping is used when any measurement group is absent. Table~\ref{tab:notation} defines the principal symbols, and Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} summarizes the computation.

\paragraph{Exposure alignment and partial records.}
Camera settings describe one exposure, whereas an IMU and vehicle bus produce streams. The records are aligned to the exposure interval, and each trajectory is sampled at $T$ normalized temporal positions. This retains direction and variation during image formation while accepting different sensor rates. For example, the same angular rate implies a smaller displacement when the exposure is shorter. The method is defined for general $T$; Appendix~\ref{app:packet} gives the finite serialization used in the experiments.

Availability and quality are represented separately. For each group $g\in\{C,I,V\}$, $a_g\in\{0,1\}$ states whether the measurement exists and $r_g\in[0,1]$ describes its reliability from timing, sensor noise, autofocus confidence, or source quality. A missing group is normalized to zero only after $a_g$ has been recorded. Consequently, zero angular rate is not confused with a missing IMU, and camera settings can remain useful when vehicle data are unavailable. These variables describe the record; they do not prescribe a restoration operation.

\paragraph{Image--sensor corruption state.}
The corruption state answers two questions: what does the image suggest, and what do the measurements support? An image encoder first estimates a bounded cause vector $z_I=g_I(I_d)$. Its coordinates describe motion, vibration, defocus, noise, weak illumination, compression, and overall severity. Independently, a physical map $h_{\rm phy}$ converts the available capture record into the same coordinate system. For motion, it begins with the rotation accumulated during exposure,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\boldsymbol{\omega}(t)\,dt,
\label{eq:motion}
\end{equation}
while exposure and gain initialize illumination and noise evidence, focus fields initialize defocus evidence, and acceleration variation contributes to vibration. Because calibration and camera--sensor geometry are imperfect, a bounded learned residual corrects the physical estimate:

\begin{equation}
z_m=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(h_{\rm phy}(\mathcal{M})+\epsilon_m R\odot\tanh \Delta h_\psi(\mathcal{M}),0,1\right),
\label{eq:sensorcode}
\end{equation}
where $R$ is assembled from the availability and reliability of the measurements supporting each cause. The residual branch is zero-initialized and bounded by $\epsilon_m$.

The model then decides, separately for each cause, how much of the sensor estimate should be trusted. A confidence vector $q$ is predicted from $R$ and the agreement between $z_I$ and $z_m$. The fused posterior is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
z_0 &= q\odot z_m+(1-q)\odot z_I,\\
z &= \operatorname{clip}\!\left(z_0+\epsilon_p\tanh P_\psi[z_I,z_m,|z_m-z_I|],0,1\right).
\end{split}
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
If a cause has no supported measurement, the corresponding $q$ is forced to zero and that coordinate follows $z_I$. The adapter state is
\begin{equation}
s=[z_I,z_m,|z_m-z_I|,q].
\label{eq:jointstate}
\end{equation}
The disagreement term makes the sensor path conditional rather than absolute. For example, a reliable angular trajectory can dominate the motion coordinate, while a focus coordinate with no lens record remains image-derived; a temporally inconsistent trajectory can be attenuated. This is how one model handles complete, partial, and unavailable records without a manual mode switch.

\paragraph{Backbone conditioning.}
Let $B$ be a multi-scale restoration backbone with feature $x_l$ at depth $l$. If $B$ exposes degradation-specific routes, supported coordinates of $z$ bias its route selection; otherwise $z$ can be used only by the adapters below. When sensor support is weak, the image-derived route is retained. Low-rank adapters then adjust feature processing at selected encoder, bottleneck, and decoder depths:
\begin{equation}
x'_l=x_l+a_l(s,q)P_l\,\sigma\!\left(D_l(Q_l\mathcal{N}(x_l);s)\right),
\label{eq:adapter}
\end{equation}
where $Q_l$ and $P_l$ are low-rank $1\times1$ projections, $D_l$ is a depthwise spatial operator with local and dilated kernels, and $\mathcal{N}$ is group normalization. State-dependent affine parameters modulate the rank-reduced features. The compatibility gate is
\begin{equation}
a_l(s,q)=\eta_l\,\sigma(w_l^\top s+b_l)\left(\delta+(1-\delta)\max_j q_j\right),
\label{eq:adaptergate}
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ retains a bounded image-conditioned path when telemetry is absent. A learned spatial gate acts inside $D_l$. The output projection is initialized to zero, so the augmented model initially reproduces its backbone rather than perturbing it arbitrarily. The formulation does not require a particular backbone; the implementation used for evaluation is specified in Section~\ref{sec:comparators} and Appendix~\ref{app:training}.

\paragraph{Task-aware learning.}
The objective has three roles: preserve the paired scene, preserve detector-relevant evidence, and calibrate the corruption state. The fidelity term is always active and provides the principal image constraint:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm fid}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i\sqrt{(I_r^i-I_c^i)^2+\varepsilon^2}
+\lambda_g\|\nabla I_r-\nabla I_c\|_1.
\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{equation}
Here $N$ is the number of image elements and $\varepsilon$ is the Charbonnier smoothing constant. The first term limits the influence of atypical crop errors; the gradient term protects narrow cracks and pothole rims.

A frozen dataset-specific detector supplies the task evidence. Following the task-driven perceptual principle~\cite{kim2024tdp}, normalized intermediate features of the restored image are matched to those of the clean target,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm TDP}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\alpha_k
\|\widehat{\Phi}_k(I_r)-\operatorname{sg}(\widehat{\Phi}_k(I_c))\|_2^2,
\label{eq:tdp}
\end{equation}
where hats denote channel-normalized features, $\alpha_k$ weights detector layer $k$, and $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients through the clean branch. A second term uses the detector's own supervised objective on restored training crops,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}=\kappa_c\mathcal{L}_{\rm cls}
+\kappa_b\mathcal{L}_{\rm box}+\kappa_f\mathcal{L}_{\rm DFL},
\label{eq:detectorloss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\rm cls}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\rm box}$, and $\mathcal{L}_{\rm DFL}$ are classification, box-regression, and distribution-focal losses and $\kappa_c,\kappa_b,\kappa_f$ are fixed detector weights. Detector parameters remain frozen in both terms, so the restorer must improve the image presented to a fixed task model rather than co-adapt a new detector.

The third role is state calibration. The image, sensor, and posterior codes are supervised by the known training corruption state $z^*$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm state}=\sum_{u\in\{z_I,z_m,z\}}\rho_u
\operatorname{SmoothL1}(u,z^*).
\label{eq:stateloss}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_u$ balances the image, sensor, and joint estimates; unsupported sensor coordinates are masked rather than assigned a guessed target. The observable physical target $z^*_{\rm phy}$ contains only quantities derivable from the training capture record. With support mask $A$, its calibration loss is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{\rm phy}={}&
\sum_{u\in\{z_I,z_m,z\}}\frac{\nu_u}{\max(1,\|A_u\|_1)}\\
&\times\sum_j A_{u,j}\operatorname{SmoothL1}
\!\left(u_{{\rm phy},j},z^*_{{\rm phy},j}\right),
\end{split}
\label{eq:physicalloss}
\end{equation}
where $\nu_u$ weights the three estimates and $A_u$ combines physical observability with cause-wise sensor support. All detector, state, and physical terms are therefore defined from training targets. The complete objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm fid}
+\lambda_t\mathcal{L}_{\rm TDP}
+\lambda_d\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}
+\lambda_s\mathcal{L}_{\rm state}
+\lambda_p\mathcal{L}_{\rm phy}.
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The terms are not introduced at full strength simultaneously. Fidelity remains active throughout training; detector terms begin after a short warm-up, and sensor perturbation is introduced through an aligned-record curriculum followed by a gradual ramp. The detector is frozen, the clean feature branch is detached, and the state losses are lower-weight calibration terms. This staged schedule prevents an early detector gradient or a noisy sensor record from displacing the image solution before the restoration path is stable. It is ordinary joint optimization after the ramps, not a set of competing backward passes.

Training also drops camera, IMU, and vehicle groups independently, perturbs available measurements, and occasionally pairs an image with an inconsistent record. These interventions teach partial-record use and rejection of unsupported telemetry. At inference the model automatically emits $I_r$; there is no manual metadata mode or post-restoration choice. Appendix~\ref{app:training} reports the frozen coefficients and schedule.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\subsection{Controlled Road-Damage Benchmarks}
IVCNZ contains pothole imagery; PCM contains pothole, crack, and manhole annotations~\cite{giordani2025roadpcm}. Source and temporal groups are separated before rendering. The training partitions contain 731 IVCNZ and 1,402 PCM source images, and the validation partitions contain 146 and 353 source images. Four independently sampled corruption families are rendered per source: motion, defocus, low light, and compound motion--low light. A corresponding virtual capture record is produced from the same image-formation event, with measurement noise, timing uncertainty, and split-disjoint vehicle trajectories. It contains noisy camera/IMU/vehicle observations, not the hidden blur kernel, scenario name, clean image, or detector label. Appendix~\ref{app:controlled_generation} gives the renderer and sensor ranges. Test identities remain inaccessible until the validation-selected checkpoint and all comparator policies are frozen.

The detector is YOLO11s~\cite{ultralytics2023yolo}, trained on the clean training split for each dataset and then frozen. Detection is evaluated on restored images with COCO-style mAP50 and mAP50--95. Clean-test detector accuracy is 0.424 mAP50 on IVCNZ and 0.334 on PCM, providing an upper reference for the corruption study. Paired fidelity is measured by PSNR and SSIM.

\subsection{Comparators and Matched Adaptation}
\label{sec:comparators}
The comparison includes NAFNet~\cite{chen2022nafnet}, InstructIR~\cite{conde2024instructir}, DFPIR~\cite{tian2025dfpir}, and DeMoE~\cite{feijoo2025demoe}. They represent an image-only architecture, instruction-guided restoration, degradation-prompted feature perturbation, and image-routed expert restoration, respectively. Each adapter imports the authors' original model class and requires complete coverage of its current tensors; no restoration layer is omitted or replaced. NAFNet uses the released width-32 GoPro architecture and tensors. InstructIR retains its language model and image-restoration network, DFPIR retains its CLIP prompt encoder and perturbation blocks, and DeMoE retains its five-expert routing architecture. The architecture audit is summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:training}.

TRACE-R instantiates its generic backbone $B$ with DeMoE because the expert routes supply a strong image-only response to motion, defocus, and illumination changes. DeMoE-auto is therefore the direct capacity control: it has the same backbone but no capture-state path or adapters. This design choice isolates the value of measured capture evidence from the value of changing the restoration family.

Every trainable method receives the same controlled training sources, balanced defect-crop stream, continuation epochs, optimizer-update budget, and frozen dataset-specific detectors. Generated training conditions can supervise a baseline's prompt or expert, but inference information differs as shown in Table~\ref{tab:inference_inputs}. DFPIR and InstructIR are condition-informed controls in the controlled experiment, whereas DeMoE-auto and NAFNet infer from the image alone. DeMoE-oracle receives the rendered condition directly as an expert-selection label and is kept separate as a non-deployable upper bound.

\subsection{CRID Native-Resolution Field Study}
The Collingwood Road Inspection Dataset (CRID) contains 4,134 native 4752$\times$3168 Sony ILX-LR1 frames acquired from a survey vehicle with SBG navigation and inertial measurements. Forty-six frames have manually checked road-defect boxes. The strict synchronized subset comprises 25 annotated frames with direct camera--SBG alignment: an earlier 12-frame segment fixes the restoration strength using the predeclared relaxed-overlap calibration score, and a disjoint later 13-frame segment containing 49 defects is evaluated once. No synthetic degradation is added, and all images and boxes remain in native coordinates. Appendix~\ref{app:crid} documents the collection platform and route.

CRID is a transfer test, not a field fine-tuning set. TRACE-R uses the same checkpoint trained jointly on controlled IVCNZ and PCM; no CRID image or annotation updates its weights. At field inference, the virtual record is replaced by per-frame Sony exposure metadata and a synchronized 200-Hz SBG window. Only a conservative residual strength is fixed on the earlier 12-frame segment and then held constant for the later test. Comparator checkpoints are likewise fixed before that test.

The frozen field detector uses the YOLO26 family~\cite{jocher2026yolo26} initialized from a public road-damage checkpoint~\cite{tamako2026yolo26rddfrdc}. Every restorer uses the same detector, resize rule, confidence floor, and class-aware suppression. Pooled AP50 over the four mapped road-defect classes is the headline field metric. AP10 is retained because it was the validation calibration score and because long crack annotations and fragmented predictions can overlap weakly even when they refer to the same defect. Unlike the controlled mAP50, pooled field AP50 is not an average of per-class AP values; the two experiments therefore use the same 0.50 localization threshold but different aggregation because the field class counts are sparse.

\section{Discussion}
Three observations are central. First, capture telemetry is useful only when it is tied to image evidence. The unavailable-record result remains competitive because $z_I$ and the backbone's image route stay active; the inconsistent-record reduction shows that the model has not learned a generic gain from an extra vector. Second, the SSIM--mAP and PSNR--mAP rankings differ. Minimizing pixel error can favour conservative smoothing, whereas detector-aware adaptation preserves defect structure. Third, the sensor state must influence both route selection and feature processing. Cause-aware routing chooses a suitable restoration regime, while the distributed adapters adjust its strength and spatial response continuously.

The physical code should be interpreted as a calibrated prior, not an exact blur kernel. Camera rotation during exposure is directly relevant to image motion, but acceleration, speed, and yaw provide context rather than depth-complete optical flow. Equation~\eqref{eq:posterior} therefore exposes reliability and disagreement to the learned posterior. This also explains why partial records are useful: reliable exposure and focus fields can contribute when IMU data are absent, while measured angular rate can contribute when lens metadata are unavailable.

The principal limitations are sensor alignment and domain coverage. Controlled corruption provides paired clean targets and observable virtual records, but cannot cover every pavement, weather, lens, and rolling-shutter interaction. CRID supplies real camera and inertial measurements, but the present detection claim is limited to its manually checked synchronized subset. The model also assumes that timing and calibration quality can be summarized by the supplied reliability fields. Large offsets, unmodelled camera--IMU extrinsics, or a detector outside the training domain can still reduce performance. In deployment, native images and capture logs should be retained for audit, restored outputs should be marked as derived data, and low-confidence detections should remain reviewable by an operator.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper introduced \tracer, a restoration front end that uses the measurements already recorded by an inspection vehicle. The method aligns camera, inertial, and vehicle records to an exposure, maps supported measurements and image evidence into a common corruption state, and conditions one multi-scale restorer according to their reliability and agreement. A staged task-aware objective preserves paired image structure while teaching the restored output to retain features and localization evidence used by a fixed road-defect detector.

Under matched data, optimization budgets, and validation-only selection, \tracer provides the strongest deployable detection result on the IVCNZ and PCM controlled tests. The same jointly trained checkpoint also achieves the highest primary AP50 on the later native-resolution CRID field block without CRID weight adaptation. Removing or contradicting the capture record reduces validation accuracy, directly supporting the role of measured capture state. These results position telemetry-conditioned restoration as a practical complement to detector development: it improves the evidence delivered to an existing detector while retaining one auditable image output. The remaining oracle gap, cross-session sensor calibration, and wider CRID annotation are the most important directions for subsequent validation.

\section{Capture Record and State Construction}
\label{app:packet}
The method in Section III accepts an inertial trajectory of general length $T$. The evaluated configuration uses $T=11$ because the field SBG unit records at 200~Hz and a 50~ms capture-centred support contains eleven samples at 5~ms spacing. Linear interpolation evaluates angular rate and acceleration at these common temporal positions. This is a sampling convention, not a learned degradation label or an intrinsic dimensionality of TRACE-R.

This choice yields 66 trajectory values and 16 context values. The context comprises log exposure, log ISO, analog gain, focal length, aperture, focus-error proxy, autofocus confidence, rolling-shutter readout, vehicle speed, vehicle yaw rate, timestamp offset, IMU noise, three modality reliabilities, and overall availability. Inertial quantities are normalized to calibrated full scale and remain signed. Camera and quality values lie in $[0,1]$; timing offset and yaw rate remain signed. The motion map integrates angular rate over exposure, whereas focus, noise, and low-light coordinates use the corresponding supported lens and camera fields. The 82 scalar values are therefore a concrete serialization of $\mathcal{M}$, not a degradation class or a direct backbone command.

Availability is indexed by the three-bit pattern $4a_C+2a_I+a_V$. Each pattern has a small zero-initialized calibration residual, but reliability remains continuous. This separation prevents a camera+IMU record from being treated as equivalent to camera+vehicle data. Measurement noise never alters availability fields, and dropped modalities remain zero after perturbation.

For CRID, packet construction matches each image timestamp to the 200-Hz SBG stream, samples the exposure-centred window, and records synchronization uncertainty. EXIF and navigation fields are normalized with statistics fixed from the training data. The controlled construction is described separately below.

\section{Controlled Corruption and Virtual Capture Records}
\label{app:controlled_generation}
Clean IVCNZ and PCM source images are rendered independently under four families. Motion uses a normalized line-spread function with odd length sampled from 7--19 pixels and angle from $[-30^\circ,30^\circ]$. Defocus uses Gaussian blur with standard deviation 1.1--3.4 pixels. Low light applies a gain of 0.32--0.70, gamma of 1.05--1.55, and additive Gaussian noise with standard deviation 2--8 in 8-bit intensity units. The compound condition first applies 5--15 pixel motion at $[-45^\circ,45^\circ]$ and then low-light gain 0.38--0.72, gamma 1.05--1.45, and noise standard deviation 2--9. Parameters are sampled per image from a split-seeded generator, so train, validation, and test source identities and realizations remain distinct.

The virtual record represents what a synchronized capture system could observe. Exposure and ISO are sampled consistently with illumination; focal length and aperture are sampled within 28--50~mm and $f/2.8$--$f/8$; focus error and autofocus confidence are noisy functions of the rendered defocus. For motion, image-plane displacement is converted to an exposure-normalized angular trajectory, then perturbed by calibrated inertial noise and timestamp offset. Acceleration, speed, and yaw context are sampled from KITTI OXTS drives partitioned independently across train, validation, and test~\cite{geiger2012kitti}. The public record contains the noisy temporal measurements, settings, availability, and reliability. The exact line kernel, blur angle and length, clean target, corruption-family name, and detector annotation are excluded from the inference record. Cause labels derived from the renderer are retained only as private training calibration targets and are absent from test inputs.

\section{Optimization and Selection Details}
\label{app:training}
The matched run uses 32 continuation epochs, 512 balanced crops per epoch, $128\times128$ patches, effective batch size four, AdamW with initial learning rate $10^{-5}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, and 4096 optimizer updates. Capture-state modules use a fivefold learning-rate multiplier because they are initialized near identity while the backbone begins from released restoration weights. Adapter rank is 24 and maximum adapter gain is 0.35. Motion, defocus, and low-light route thresholds are fixed from validation before the controlled test.

In Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective}, $(\lambda_g,\lambda_t,\lambda_d,\lambda_s,\lambda_p)=(0.15,0.20,0.10,0.10,0.05)$ and $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$. The image, sensor, and joint state weights are $\rho=(0.5,0.5,1.0)$ and $\nu=(0.25,0.25,0.50)$. Detector features are taken from the second and fourth backbone stages. The detector remains frozen, and the clean feature path does not store gradients. Detector-aware terms warm up for one epoch. Defect-centred crops are sampled with probability 0.6, and cross-quality patch mixing is applied to the supervised detector branch with probability 0.25.

Capture-record robustness reaches its full setting after four aligned-record epochs and a four-epoch ramp. At full strength, modality dropout is 0.10, Gaussian measurement noise is 0.005 in normalized units, and record-mismatch probability is 0.05. Scenario-label teacher forcing is zero. This schedule separates three optimization roles: fidelity establishes a stable image path, detector terms subsequently protect task evidence, and calibration terms learn how the state should respond to supported or contradictory records. After the ramp, a single weighted update optimizes the complete objective in Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective}. Validation mAP50 alone selects the checkpoint.

All five trainable methods complete the same 32-epoch, 4096-update continuation. The release records their complete SHA-256 identities. The selected TRACE-R checkpoint is epoch 8 because it has the highest joint validation mAP50; the remaining epochs are retained in the training history and were not used to stop the run early.

Table~\ref{tab:architecture_audit} records the exact upstream architectures. Every accepted checkpoint covers all tensors used by its current source model. DFPIR follows its official evaluation loader: twelve obsolete attention scalars retained in the released checkpoint are excluded because they are absent from the current source, while all 590 current tensors are present and shape-compatible. NAFNet is adapted in full precision because its deep activation-free stage is numerically unstable under mixed precision with the detector-aware objective; its data, crops, update count, loss weights, and validation criterion remain matched.

\section{Reproducibility and Governance}
The project release includes source code, capture-record definitions, dataset builders, training configuration, selected checkpoint hashes, frozen split hashes, test ledgers, and the scripts that produce the tables and figures. The repository will also host the de-identified CRID image, telemetry, synchronization, and annotation release.
